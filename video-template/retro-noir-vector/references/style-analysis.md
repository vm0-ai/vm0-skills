## Style characteristics

- **Surface:** hard-edged 2D color planes with visible fine mottling/print grain. The grain sits within flat fills; it does not create thick paper layers, cut edges or realistic material depth. Even the final eye is flat vector-like artwork, not a photographic insert.
- **Palette:** cream, black and navy supply the main light/dark structure. Crimson, burnt orange and mustard create hot accents; dusty pink, slate gray and muted blue/cyan support scene changes. Usually a subset dominates each tableau. Preserve value contrast and role consistency rather than forcing every color into every scene.
- **Composition:** each scene reads as a deliberately cropped poster. Tiny moving figures share space with huge letters, faces, wedges, stripes or portals. Negative space and hard diagonals are active elements, not empty padding.
- **Typography:** opening title fragments are heavy, cropped sans capitals oriented with vertical bands. The later D is a filled, high-contrast serif glyph with a large counter, not outline lettering. These are two different typographic roles: title rhythm and monumental architecture.
- **Figures and shadows:** small flat silhouettes carry action. Only a few features or accent colors identify them. Long solid shadows enlarge their graphic presence and follow the local scene geometry. The cast can contain more than one figure.
- **Camera and space:** views are substantially locked within tableaux. Depth is drawn with overlap, scale, diagonals and one-point perspective. Scenes can change viewpoint and scale through editing; that does not require a camera flying through a 3D world.
- **Transitions:** cross-dissolves visibly superimpose outgoing and incoming shapes. Matching edges, large color regions, foreground placement or vanishing-point areas give the overlaps structure. Transient double exposure is part of the look; unintended persistent clones are a generation defect.
- **Rhythm and mood:** measured holds alternate with roughly half-second dissolves and more abrupt scale changes. Several scenes use lateral walking/running, while the opening and glyph scene also use vertical motion. Suspenseful, theatrical, witty graphic tension is more defining than a literal chase plot.

## Style invariants and optional content

Keep flat printed color planes, strong light/dark separation, a reduced silhouette vocabulary, oversized architectural shapes, purposeful scale changes and visually connected dissolves.

One subject moving in one direction can simplify a new brief, but the source does not maintain one cast member, one axis or one baseline throughout. Preserve continuity within a passage; introduce a new axis with a clear compositional handoff. The ending echoes an earlier eye, not the opening title, so choose any deliberate earlier-to-later motif connection.

Cats, noir costumes, source-specific confrontation props, vending machines, the letter D, the eye and the exact scene order are content choices. Translate their geometric functions into the user's subject matter. They are not required ingredients of this visual style.

## Transfer example

For a night-travel film, use a small umbrella silhouette, a large station clock, striped platforms, a monumental initial and repeated portal frames. Carry the clock's circle into a later moon, and continue the umbrella's local path through each dissolve. Preserve cream/dark contrast and diagonal shadows while replacing all source characters and locations.

## Recognition check

Every stable frame should read as a strong graphic poster. In motion, scale changes and dissolving flat geometry should carry the story. A soft paper diorama, a photoreal close-up, identical centered scenes, or a universal one-axis travel rule would lose important properties of the reference.
